# Career Progression, Promotion and Compensation

## The generic progression ladder
Most knowledge-work careers follow a similar arc, with different titles:
1. **Entry / junior** (0-2 yrs): execute defined tasks with guidance; learn the craft.
2. **Mid-level** (2-5 yrs): own workstreams independently; deliver reliably.
3. **Senior** (5-8 yrs): own complex problems, mentor others, influence decisions.
4. **Lead / manager / principal** (8+ yrs): lead teams or become a deep individual expert.

## Individual contributor (IC) vs management track
- **IC track:** deepen expertise; titles like senior -> staff -> principal -> distinguished.
  Best if you love the craft and want influence without people management.
- **Management track:** lead people; titles like manager -> senior manager -> director -> VP.
  Best if you enjoy developing people, strategy and coordination.
Many companies let you switch between tracks; choosing one is not permanent.

## What actually drives promotion
- **Scope:** the size and ambiguity of problems you own, growing over time.
- **Impact:** measurable results for users or the business.
- **Visibility:** decision-makers know what you did (this is legitimate, not bragging).
- **Reliability:** consistently delivering, especially under pressure.
- **Leadership behaviours:** mentoring, raising the bar, unblocking others.
- **Sponsorship:** someone senior advocates for you in rooms you are not in.

## Getting promoted: practical steps
1. Understand the next level's expectations and find them in writing if possible.
2. Build a **brag document** with concrete achievements and numbers.
3. Take on a stretch project that demonstrates next-level scope.
4. Ask your manager for specific, dated feedback and a plan.
5. Ensure at least one senior person knows your work.

## Compensation components
- **Base salary** — fixed pay.
- **Bonus** — performance or company-linked.
- **Equity** — stock options or RSUs; value depends on company stage and vesting.
- **Benefits** — health, pension/retirement, insurance, leave, learning budget.
Compare **total compensation**, not base alone, and adjust for cost of living.

## Salary expectations and negotiation
- **Research ranges** using public sources (levels.fyi, Glassdoor, national salary surveys,
  industry reports) and local cost of living.
- **Negotiate** politely with market data; the highest leverage moment is the offer stage.
- **Consider the whole package:** level, growth, manager quality, learning, flexibility.
- Early career, prioritise **learning and signal**; mid-career, prioritise **scope and comp**.

## Typical progression timelines (rough, not guarantees)
- Analyst -> senior analyst: 2-4 years.
- Software engineer -> senior: 3-6 years; senior -> staff: 3-5 more.
- Consultant -> manager: 3-5 years.
- These vary widely by company, performance and market conditions.

## Careers are rarely linear
Many people move sideways, take a pay cut for a pivot, return to study, or change industry.
A useful mental model is **career capital**: every role should add skills, network and
reputation that increase your future options. Optimise for compounding career capital,
especially before you have heavy financial commitments.

## When to leave a role
Consider moving on when: you have stopped learning, your growth is blocked, compensation is
persistently below market, or the environment harms your wellbeing. Leave *toward* something
(clear next step) rather than only *away* from a problem.
