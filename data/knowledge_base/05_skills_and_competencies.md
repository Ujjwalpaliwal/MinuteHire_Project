# Skills and Competencies

## Technical vs. transferable skills
**Technical (hard) skills** are job-specific and learnable: programming, SQL, financial
modelling, Figma, statistics. **Transferable (soft) skills** apply across roles:
communication, problem-solving, collaboration, time management, adaptability.

Careers are increasingly decided by the combination. Two candidates with the same hard
skills are separated by communication, ownership and reliability.

## The core transferable skills employers want
1. **Communication** — explaining complex ideas simply, in writing and in speech.
2. **Problem-solving** — breaking ambiguous problems into parts and testing solutions.
3. **Collaboration** — working across functions and handling disagreement.
4. **Ownership** — taking initiative and seeing things through without being told.
5. **Learning agility** — picking up new tools and domains quickly.
6. **Data literacy** — reading, questioning and communicating with data.

## How to build skills efficiently
- **Deliberate practice:** work slightly beyond your current ability with fast feedback.
- **Project-based learning:** build real artefacts rather than only watching courses.
- **Spaced repetition and active recall:** for retention of fundamentals.
- **Teach to learn:** write or explain what you learned (blogs, talks, notes).
- **Public proof of work:** a portfolio, GitHub, blog or talks make skills visible.

## Assessing your own strengths and weaknesses
- **Strengths** are where you learn fast, get energy and outperform peers.
- **Weaknesses** are areas that block your goals — not every low skill needs fixing.
- Use structured reflection: list what tasks gave you energy vs. drained you over the last
  months, and what feedback you repeatedly receive.
- Ask 3-5 people who have worked with you for one strength and one improvement.

## Skill gaps and how to close them
For a target role, collect 5-10 job postings and tally the required skills. Rank them by
frequency. For each gap you choose to close:
1. Pick the smallest useful project that exercises the skill.
2. Give it a deadline and a deliverable.
3. Get feedback from someone more experienced.
4. Add it to your portfolio as evidence.

## Technical literacy that compounds across careers
Even non-coding roles benefit from:
- **SQL** — querying data directly.
- **Basic scripting (Python)** — automating small tasks.
- **Version control (Git)** — collaborating on any digital work.
- **APIs and how the web works** — understanding what is possible.
- **Spreadsheets to an advanced level** — modelling and analysis.

## Signs you are developing a strength
- You receive unprompted recognition for it.
- You are sought out for it by teammates.
- You can improve at it faster than peers with the same practice.
- It energises rather than drains you.

## Competency frameworks and interviews
Most structured interviews assess a fixed set of competencies: communication,
collaboration, ownership, problem-solving, dealing with ambiguity and domain skill. Prepare
**STAR stories** (Situation, Task, Action, Result) that map to each. Reuse and re-angle the
same experiences for different competencies.

## Building a personal learning system
- Set a quarterly theme (one skill to deepen).
- Keep a running "brag document" of achievements and metrics.
- Review monthly: what did I learn, what did I ship, who did I help?
- Maintain a small network of peers and mentors for feedback.
