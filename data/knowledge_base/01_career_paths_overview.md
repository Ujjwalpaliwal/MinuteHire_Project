# Career Paths: Overview and How to Choose

## What a career path actually is
A career path is a sequence of roles that build on one another in skills, scope and
responsibility. Students often think of a career as a single job title. In practice a
career is a direction with many entry points, side routes and return points. The goal of
career planning is not to pick the "perfect" job on the first attempt, but to choose a
direction that lets you learn transferable skills while you gather more information.

## The three questions that drive career choice
Career counselling frameworks repeatedly reduce the decision to three questions:
1. **What can I do well?** (skills, strengths, aptitudes)
2. **What do I enjoy / care about?** (interests, values, motivation)
3. **Where is there demand?** (industries, roles, growth, pay)

The best options sit at the intersection of all three. If you only optimise for interest
you may struggle to get hired; if you only optimise for demand you may burn out. Treat the
intersection as a hypothesis to test rather than a permanent identity.

## Broad career clusters for graduates
- **Technology & Software** — software engineering, data, AI/ML, cloud, DevOps, security, QA.
- **Product & Design** — product management, UX/UI, product design, technical program management.
- **Business & Operations** — consulting, finance, marketing, sales, operations, HR, supply chain.
- **Creative & Content** — writing, media, games, content strategy, design, developer relations.
- **Healthcare & Science** — research, bioinformatics, public health, clinical roles.
- **Public sector & Education** — teaching, policy, academia, civil services, NGOs.

## Career path vs. job vs. industry
- **Job** = the specific role you do now (e.g. junior analyst).
- **Career path** = the progression across roles (analyst -> associate -> manager).
- **Industry** = the sector you work in (e.g. fintech, healthcare, edtech).

You can change any one of these independently. Changing industries often lets you keep
your function; changing functions often lets you keep your industry. This is a useful lever
when you feel stuck.

## The T-shaped skill model
Modern career advice favours "T-shaped" people: broad working knowledge across many areas
(the top of the T) plus deep expertise in one area (the stem). Early in a career, spend
most energy building the stem, but keep enough breadth to collaborate across functions.
Product-adjacent roles are a good example: a product manager has deep product judgment but
needs enough technical, design and business literacy to work with each team.

## Entry points matter more than titles
For a first role, prioritise:
1. **Learning velocity** — do you work with people you can learn from?
2. **Skill transferability** — will these skills be valuable elsewhere?
3. **Signal** — does the brand/role make the next step easier?
4. **Compensation** — important but usually a lagging reward; don't over-optimise at entry.

## Common myths, corrected
- *"You must decide once."* Most people change direction several times; 3-5 year horizons
  are more realistic than 30-year plans.
- *"Your degree fixes your career."* Degrees open doors but skills and projects often
  decide outcomes.
- *"You need to be passionate about work."* Sustained interest and competence matter more
  than an initial spark of passion.
- *"I have to code to work in tech."* Many high-impact technology roles are non-coding or
  light-coding: product, data analysis, design, sales engineering, developer relations.

## A practical decision process
1. List 3-5 directions that fit the three questions.
2. For each, identify one cheap experiment: an online course, a small project, a
   conversation with someone doing the job, a shadowing day, or a freelance task.
3. Run the experiments over 4-8 weeks.
4. Double down on what felt energising *and* where you were able to make progress.
5. Re-evaluate every 6-12 months; careers are iterative, not one-shot.
