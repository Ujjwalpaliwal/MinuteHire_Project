# Technology Careers for Students Who Do Not Love Hardcore Coding

Many computer science and IT students enjoy building products, solving user problems and
technology in general, but do not want to spend their career deep in algorithms and
low-level implementation. This document lists strong alternatives and how to enter them.

## Product Management (PM)
Product managers decide *what* to build and *why*, prioritise features, write requirements
and coordinate engineering, design and business. It rewards communication, structured
thinking and user empathy far more than coding ability.
- **Very useful:** basic technical literacy (APIs, databases, system basics), data analysis,
  writing crisp documents, running user interviews.
- **Entry routes:** APM (associate PM) programmes, internal transfer from engineering,
  support, analytics, consulting or a founder/startup role.
- **Signals employers look for:** a shipped side project, a product case study, clear
  writing (blog posts, PRDs) and evidence of cross-functional collaboration.

## Solutions / Sales Engineer, Forward Deployed Engineer
You sit between the product and the customer, doing demos, integrations, proofs of concept
and light scripting. Coding is a tool rather than the centre of the job.
- **Very useful:** scripting (Python/JS), SQL, REST APIs, cloud basics, presentation skills.
- **Entry routes:** support engineering -> solutions engineering; graduate programmes at
  B2B software companies; customer success with a technical bent.

## Developer Relations (DevRel) / Developer Advocate
You build a community around a product through demos, tutorials, talks and content. It
combines light coding with writing, teaching and public speaking.
- **Very useful:** one strong technical domain (e.g. web, ML, mobile), writing, video,
  conference speaking.
- **Entry routes:** start by publishing tutorials and talks, contribute to open source,
  then move into a DevRel role or internal advocacy.

## Data Analyst / Business Analyst (light-coding)
Analyse data, build dashboards, find insights and communicate them. SQL plus one tool
(Excel, Power BI, Tableau, Python/pandas) covers most requirements.
- **Very useful:** SQL, spreadsheet modelling, statistics intuition, storytelling with data.
- **Entry routes:** analytics graduate schemes, ops/support roles with a data angle,
  internal analytics transfers.

## UX / Product Design and UX Research
Design how products look, feel and flow; research studies users to inform decisions.
Designers prototype rather than write production code.
- **Very useful:** wireframing and prototyping (Figma), design thinking, user research,
  accessibility, a portfolio.
- **Entry routes:** design bootcamps, freelance projects, internal transfers from front-end
  or support.

## Technical Program / Project Management (TPM)
Coordinate complex technical projects, dependencies and timelines across teams. You need to
understand engineering but you are not the primary coder.
- **Very useful:** planning, stakeholder communication, risk management, light technical
  understanding, delivery track record.

## Quality Assurance / Test Engineering and Automation
Ensure software works correctly, increasingly through automation and tooling.
- **Very useful:** test design, one scripting language, CI tools, attention to detail.
- **Entry routes:** manual QA -> automation; SDET roles.

## Technical Writing / Documentation
Turn complex technology into clear documentation, guides and API references.
- **Very useful:** excellent writing, ability to read code, empathy for the reader.

## Cybersecurity GRC / Analyst (non-pentest)
Governance, risk, compliance, security awareness and analysis require less low-level
coding than exploit development.

## How to choose among these
Ask: do I want to be closer to **users** (PM, design, research), **customers** (solutions,
sales engineering, customer success), **content and community** (DevRel, technical writing),
or **data** (analysis)? Each cluster has a different day-to-day texture. Run a small
experiment in the cluster that attracts you most — a case study, a dashboard, a tutorial, a
design — and use that as both a decision test and a portfolio artefact.

## The "enjoy building, hate hardcore coding" profile
This is a very common and marketable profile. Recommended directions in rough order of fit:
1. Product Management / APM
2. Solutions Engineering / Forward Deployed Engineer
3. Developer Relations / Developer Advocacy
4. Product Design / UX
5. Technical Program Management
6. Data / Business Analysis
7. Technical Writing / Documentation

Your coding ability is not wasted in any of these; it becomes a **competitive advantage**
that most non-technical candidates in these fields do not have. Framing matters: describe
yourself as someone who can build and also communicate, not as someone who "failed" to be a
pure engineer.
