# Career Counselling Frameworks and Psychology

## Purpose of career counselling
Career counselling helps a person make informed, self-consistent decisions about work. Good
counselling is exploratory and client-centred: it surfaces the person's own values,
interests and constraints rather than prescribing a single "correct" job.

## Well-established frameworks
- **Holland's RIASEC model:** people and work environments can be described by six types —
  Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional. Satisfaction is
  higher when person and environment types match. Useful for generating direction ideas.
- **Ikigai:** the intersection of what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs
  and what you can be paid for. Useful as a reflection prompt, not a precise formula.
- **Super's life-span, life-space theory:** careers develop through stages (growth,
  exploration, establishment, maintenance, decline) and multiple life roles interact.
- **Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT):** self-efficacy beliefs and outcome expectations
  strongly shape career choices — building confidence through small wins matters.
- **Planned happenstance (Krumboltz):** careers are shaped by unplanned events; the skill is
  to stay curious, persistent and open so you can capitalise on opportunities.

## Values, interests, personality, skills (VIPS)
A common intake structure:
- **Values** — what matters (autonomy, impact, security, creativity, status, balance).
- **Interests** — what you gravitate toward.
- **Personality** — how you prefer to work (introversion/extraversion, structure/ambiguity).
- **Skills** — what you can do and can credibly claim.

## The counselling conversation
Effective career counselling uses:
- **Open questions** — "What part of that did you enjoy most?"
- **Reflection** — repeating back the person's meaning to confirm understanding.
- **Paraphrasing and summaries** — consolidating what has been said.
- **Normalising** — reducing anxiety by noting that uncertainty is common.
- **Action planning** — turning insight into small, testable next steps.

## Decision-making under uncertainty
- Treat career choices as **experiments**, not verdicts.
- Use **small bets**: low-cost actions that generate information quickly.
- Beware **sunk-cost** thinking: past investment should not trap you in a bad fit.
- Beware **maximising**: chasing the single best option can cause paralysis; "good enough
  plus reversible" is often wiser.

## Common psychological blockers
- **Impostor feelings:** competence doubts despite evidence. Counter with a brag document
  and external feedback.
- **Comparison:** benchmarking against others' highlight reels. Compare to your own past.
- **Perfectionism:** delaying action until certainty arrives, which never does.
- **Analysis paralysis:** too many options. Reduce to 3 and run experiments.
- **Fear of regret:** worry about choosing "wrong". Most choices are recoverable.

## Wellbeing and sustainable careers
- Burnout is driven by unsustainable workload, low autonomy, poor fit and lack of meaning.
- Sustainable careers balance challenge with recovery, and align work with at least some
  core values.
- Career changes are normal and can be healthy; a "zigzag" path often builds unique value.

## Ethical guardrails for an AI counsellor
An AI career counsellor should: give balanced, non-directive guidance; avoid promising
outcomes; avoid discriminating or stereotyping; encourage the person's own decision-making;
flag when professional (mental-health, legal, financial) advice is needed; and be
transparent that it is an assistant, not a licensed counsellor.
